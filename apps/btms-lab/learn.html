1 · The problem

An electric-vehicle battery heats up while it works. Too hot means faster ageing and, at worst, thermal runaway. Mane et al. (Journal of Energy Storage, 2026) built a cooler with two parts: a phase change material (PCM), a wax that soaks up heat while it melts, and a pulsating heat pipe (PHP), a thin tube that carries heat to the air. They tested three waxes and two cooling modes, then trained machine-learning models to predict how well the module cools.

This lab asks three things the paper leaves open: does the model work for a wax it has never seen, does adding physics help, and which wax would be best on a hot day or a longer ride?

2 · The experiment and the data

  • Six substitute 18650 cells (aluminium with a 2 W heater each, the heat of a real cell at 3C discharge), arranged in two offset rows.
  • PCM options: none, OM42, OM48, paraffin. Cooling at the heat-pipe condenser: natural (still air, 27 °C) or forced (fan).
  • Temperature recorded every second for 1,200 s (a 3C discharge lasts 20 minutes).
rows = 1201 time steps × 6 cells × 2 cooling modes × 4 PCM options = 57,648

Each row has four inputs, PCM, Cooling, Time, Location, and one output, the cooling efficiency η. The wax properties measured by DSC (paper, Table 2) are in pcm_properties_table2.csv.

Code: btms/data.py (loading and checks) and btms/physics.py → simulated_dataset() (the simulated stand-in with the same shape).

3 · Cooling efficiency η

Instead of predicting temperature directly, the paper predicts a 0-to-1 score:

η = (T_max − T) / (T_max − T_min) T_max = 59.86 °C, T_min = 27.06 °C (hottest and coldest readings)

Example: a cell at 43.46 °C has η = (59.86 − 43.46) / 32.8 = 0.50. η = 1 means as cold as the coldest reading; η = 0 as hot as the hottest. Because the formula is linear, any error in η converts back to degrees: error_°C = error_η × 32.8. This lab reports errors in °C because that is what an engineer needs.

def eta_to_temperature(eta, t_max=59.86, t_min=27.06):
    return t_max - eta * (t_max - t_min)

5 · Honest testing: three ways to split

A model is scored on data it did not train on. How that data is chosen decides what the score means.

ProtocolHowQuestion it answers
Random (paper)shuffle all rows, 80% train, 20% testCan it fill gaps inside curves it has already seen?
Leave-one-PCM-outtrain on all waxes but one, test on the held-out wax; repeat for eachDoes it work for a new material?
Future timetrain on t ≤ 900 s, test on t > 900 sCan it extrapolate forward in time?

Why random splitting flatters the model. The reading at second 500 and the reading at second 501 are almost identical. A random split puts one in training and the other in testing, so the test is like an exam where you have seen the answers. On the simulated data, gradient boosting scores R² 0.999 with a random split and R² −0.82 when the wax is new: worse than predicting the average.

def leave_one_pcm(df):
    for pcm in df.pcm.unique():          # 'none' is kept in training by default
        test  = indices where df.pcm == pcm
        train = indices where df.pcm != pcm
        yield train, test

6 · Metrics: R² and RMSE

R² = 1 − Σ(y − ŷ)² / Σ(y − ȳ)² 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than the average, < 0 = worse than the average RMSE = √( mean((y − ŷ)²) ) typical error, in the same unit as y

On one fixed test set a lower RMSE always means a higher R² (both depend on the same squared errors). That is why a pair like "ANN: R² 0.9981, RMSE 0.0012; GB: R² 0.9991, RMSE 0.0059" cannot come from a single test set: it is worth asking how each was computed.

Code: btms/metrics.py.

7 · Physics features

The paper gives the wax as a code (OM42 = 3, say). A new wax is just an unseen number, so the model cannot reason about it. Here each wax is described by what it is:

time, forced (0/1), core_cell (0/1), has_pcm (0/1), first_deviation_°C, onset_°C, latent_heat_J/g, k_solid_W/mK, cp_solid_kJ/kgK, rho_solid_kg/m³

A new wax becomes a new point in property space that the model can place. On the simulated data, gradient boosting on a held-out wax goes from R² −0.82 (codes) to R² 0.88 (properties). With only three waxes, though, the properties are perfectly correlated with each other, so the data cannot say which property matters: that needs more materials.

Code: btms/features.py.

8 · The energy balance (physics model)

Treat one cell and its share of wax as a single lump with one temperature T:

C_eff(T) · dT/dt = Q − G · (T − T_amb) heat stored = heat made − heat removed Q = 2 W (+ extra for core cells C2, C5, which receive heat from neighbours) G = G_pipe(natural or forced) + g_k · k_PCM heat-pipe path + conduction through the wax C_eff = C_cell + m_PCM · [cp + L · melt(T)] "apparent heat capacity"

Melting as extra heat capacity. While wax melts it absorbs its latent heat L without warming. We spread L over the melting range with a bell curve centred between the DSC first-deviation and onset temperatures:

melt(T) = exp(−½ ((T − T_c)/w)²) / (w √(2π)) integrates to 1, so the total extra heat is exactly m·L

Solving it. Step forward one second at a time (explicit Euler):

T[0] = T_amb
for i in range(1200):
    C = C_cell + m_pcm * (cp + L * melt(T[i]))
    T[i+1] = T[i] + (Q - G * (T[i] - T_amb)) / C

Checks in the tests: with no cooling the rise equals Q·t/C exactly; a PCM keeps the cell cooler than none; the fan lowers the temperature further. Code: btms/physics.py; browser version btms-model.js → simulateCell().

9 · The PINN (physics-informed neural network)

Network. Input: time (scaled to 0–1) and the 8 configuration features; two hidden layers of 64 tanh neurons; output: temperature.

T̂(t, x) = T_amb + 30 · NN(t/1200, x/scale)

Loss: three parts.

L = w_data · mean( (T̂ − T_measured)² ) fit the measurements + w_phys · mean( r² ), r = [C_eff(T̂)·dT̂/dt − (Q − G·(T̂ − T_amb))] / Q obey the energy balance + w_ic · mean( T̂(0, x)² in scaled units ) start at room temperature

Automatic differentiation gives dT̂/dt exactly from the network, so the residual r can be computed at any time point, called a collocation point, even where there is no measurement.

Learnable physics. The constants C_cell, m_PCM, G_natural, G_forced, g_k, the core-cell factors and the melt width are trained together with the network weights. They are shared by all waxes; each wax enters only through its measured properties.

Why it generalises. The physics term is evaluated for every configuration, including a held-out wax whose temperatures were never used. The network must produce a curve for that wax that obeys the energy balance with the constants learned from the other waxes. That is the information a plain network does not have.

for step in range(3000):                      # Adam, learning rate 3e-3, cosine schedule
    loss = mse(net(t_batch, x_batch), T_batch)                       # data
    t_c  = uniform(0, 1, 2048).requires_grad_()                       # collocation times
    T    = T_amb + 30 * net(t_c, x_all_configs)
    dTdt = autograd.grad(T.sum(), t_c, create_graph=True)[0] / 1200
    r    = phys.residual(T, dTdt, raw_features)                      # energy balance
    loss = loss + w_phys * mean(r**2) + w_ic * mean(net(0, x)**2)
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()

Fair comparison. Setting w_phys = 0 gives the same network without physics. On the simulated data, trained without any OM42 data and tested on OM42: PINN R² 0.989 (error 0.39 °C) against R² 0.54 (2.55 °C) for the plain network. The constants it learned (for example C_cell 42.1 J/K against the true 40) are close to the values that generated the data, which shows the inverse problem is solved, not just fitted.

Code: btms/pinn.py; browser forward pass btms-model.js → predictTemperature().

10 · Uncertainty: conformal intervals

Turn any model's single prediction into a range with a coverage guarantee:

  1. Keep a calibration set the model did not train on and compute its errors |y − ŷ|.
  2. Let q be the ⌈(n+1)·0.9⌉-th smallest error.
  3. Report ŷ ± q. If new data resembles the calibration data, the range contains the truth at least 90% of the time.

The catch: the guarantee is only as honest as the calibration set. Calibrated on random rows, the intervals were ±0.27 °C and covered only 31% of a new wax; calibrated on a different wax, ±2.28 °C and 64%. With only three waxes, even that falls short of 90%. More materials are needed for trustworthy intervals.

Code: btms/conformal.py.

11 · What drives predictions: permutation importance

Shuffle one input column in the test data and measure how much the error grows. Big growth means the model relies on that input. It is measured on held-out data and works the same for every model.

Pitfall found while building this: if the test set contains one wax only, every wax property is constant there, so shuffling it changes nothing and its importance reads zero by construction. The lab therefore measures importance on the end of every run (all waxes present).

Code: btms/explain.py, run by run_design.py.

12 · PCM design explorer

  1. Learn the physical constants from the data with the PINN.
  2. Put them into the energy balance.
  3. Simulate hypothetical waxes: melting point 30–66 °C, latent heat 150–300 J/g, ambient 27 °C or 40 °C, ride 20 or 60 minutes.
  4. Pick the melting point whose worst peak temperature over the ambient conditions is lowest (a robust choice).

Why a hot day matters. The module starts at ambient temperature. A wax melting at 34 °C is already liquid on a 40 °C day, so its latent heat is used up before the ride starts. On the simulated, calibrated model the robust best melting point is about 42 °C for a 20-minute ride and about 48 °C for a 60-minute ride. These are hypotheses for the lab to test, not results.

Code: btms/design.py; try it on the live model.

14 · Limits and honesty

  • All results here come from simulated data generated by the same kind of model the PINN uses, which makes the PINN's job easier than it will be on real measurements.
  • The lumped model ignores temperature differences inside a cell and the pulsating behaviour of the heat pipe.
  • Three waxes are too few to separate the effect of each property or to calibrate uncertainty well.
  • The authors' public repository contains a 100-row sample; the study needs their full dataset.
